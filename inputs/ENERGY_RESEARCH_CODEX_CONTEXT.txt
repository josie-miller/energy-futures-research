PROMPT 1 — FUTURE ENERGY MIX ON EARTH AND IN SPACE

1A. Demand: how much energy will be needed?
- What are historical primary energy, final energy, electricity consumption, and electricity generation? How do their accounting boundaries differ?
- Which sectors use energy today: industry, buildings, transportation, data centers, and other uses? Which categories overlap?
- Where is demand growing geographically, and why?
- How do population, incomes, industrial output, efficiency, electrification, climate, and new uses affect demand?
- Which existing uses move from fuels to electricity? How does increased conversion efficiency change total energy requirements?
- How much growth comes from data centers, EVs, cooling, industrial electrification, hydrogen, or other emerging loads? Which estimates are evidenced versus speculative?
- What do credible scenarios imply for 2030, 2040, and 2050? How sensitive are results to the main assumptions?
- What is the difference between annual demand growth and growth in peak load? How much demand can shift in time or location?

1B. Supply: what generation must be built?
- How much existing generation remains available, and how much retires?
- How much new annual generation is needed for growth, replacement, and any specified decarbonization objective?
- What installed capacity, construction pace, storage, and network infrastructure does this imply?
- Which technologies can meet electricity, heat, fuel, and feedstock needs?
- Compare solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, wave, tidal, fission, and fusion; retain relevant incumbent fossil technologies as benchmarks.
- Distinguish commercial deployments, demonstrations, prototypes, and concepts. What has each actually demonstrated?
- What are resource availability, geographic limits, capacity factors, variability, controllability, reliability, and environmental constraints?
- What are current costs and plausible future costs, including capital, financing, fuel, maintenance, replacement, and decommissioning?
- What limits manufacturing and deployment speed: materials, factories, skilled labor, permits, connections, or unresolved engineering?

1C. Mix: what combination serves demand at the lowest acceptable cost?
- What reliability and emissions requirements should the comparison satisfy?
- Which technologies complement one another across hours, seasons, and locations?
- How much storage, transmission, backup, overbuilding, and flexible demand does each portfolio require?
- Which portfolios minimize total delivered system cost under those constraints?
- How do results change with geography, weather years, financing, construction delays, technology learning, and fuel prices?
- Under what measurable conditions does ocean energy win a share? What would disprove that case?
- What can be deployed in time, even if another technology eventually has lower costs?

1D. Space: does the answer change by environment?
- Orbit: which orbit, mission, lifetime, load, eclipse pattern, launch mass, radiation environment, heat-rejection requirement, and maintenance constraint?
- Moon: which site and illumination pattern, darkness duration, dust, temperature range, landing mass, storage requirement, and resupply plan?
- Mars: which location, seasons, dust conditions, load, transport constraints, local resources, and redundancy requirements?
- How do solar plus storage, fission, and other technically plausible systems compare on delivered mass, dependable power, lifetime energy, and mission risk?
- Is power consumed locally or transmitted elsewhere? Include transmission equipment and losses if relevant.
- Which technologies are deployable within the mission timeframe, and which remain speculative?

Expected outputs: demand scenarios, generation requirements, a technology comparison, conditional least-cost portfolios, and separate space assessments. Do not extrapolate terrestrial $/MWh rankings directly to space.

PROMPT 2 — FISSION/FUSION DEPLOYMENT, COMPANIES, FINANCING, UTILITIES

2A. What would deployment at scale mean?
- Define first-of-a-kind and nth-of-a-kind in measurable terms: repeated design, completed projects, operating experience, costs, construction time, and annual deployment capacity.
- Separate existing reactor life extensions, new large reactors, light-water SMRs, advanced fission designs, and different fusion approaches.
- Which engineering, fuel-cycle, licensing, construction, and operating milestones remain for each?
- For fusion, distinguish plasma results from sustained net electricity exported after total plant energy use, maintainability, and commercial operation.
- What evidence demonstrates repeatability rather than a single successful experiment or plant?

2B. Which companies and projects could lead?
- What is actually operating, under construction, financed, licensed, contracted, or only announced?
- Which firms have credible technical evidence, experienced teams, qualified suppliers, manufacturing capability, customers, and sufficient financial runway?
- Which company claims can be independently corroborated?
- How do economics change with construction delays, interest during construction, availability, fuel, maintenance, plant lifetime, and decommissioning?
- Are projected savings from modularity, factories, and repeated builds supported by evidence?
- Where are the supply bottlenecks: enrichment or specialized fuels, components, materials, crews, testing, or regulatory capacity?

2C. What capital stack and adoption mechanism work?
- What funds research, demonstration, first commercial deployment, and repeated deployment?
- What roles can grants, venture equity, strategic investors, government guarantees, customer commitments, utility balance sheets, and project debt play?
- Which risks can lenders accept, and which need guarantees, equity, contracts, or public support?
- Who bears overruns, delays, performance shortfalls, fuel risk, and changing demand?
- How do regulated utilities, competitive generators, data-center buyers, and industrial customers make different decisions?
- How do cost, completion date, reliability, regulation, and alternatives affect adoption?
- What realistic deployment curves follow from these constraints, and what evidence would invalidate them?

Expected outputs: separate fission and fusion evidence maps, deployment scenarios, company comparisons, financing structures, and utility adoption analysis. An announced purchase agreement is not the same as a financed operating project.

PROMPT 3 — BATTERY LEARNING AND GRID-SCALE BOTTLENECKS

3A. What service and cost are being measured?
- Is the use frequency response, daily shifting, overnight supply, multiday resilience, or seasonal balancing?
- Which chemistries and alternative storage technologies fit each use?
- Are quoted costs for cells, packs, installed systems, or lifetime delivered energy?
- How do efficiency, degradation, usable depth of discharge, calendar/cycle life, replacement, safety, and financing affect economics?

3B. What is the learning rate?
- What consistent historical price and cumulative-production data exist for each chemistry and system boundary?
- How much cost change reflects learning versus mineral prices, subsidies, manufacturing utilization, product mix, or exchange rates?
- What is the observed cost reduction per doubling of cumulative production, and how uncertain is it?
- Does a constant learning rate remain credible as technology matures?
- What learning transfers from EV batteries to grid storage, and what does not?

3C. What limits manufacturing and deployment?
- Are bottlenecks in mining, refining, active materials, cells, packs, power electronics, transformers, labor, or factory yield?
- How much announced factory capacity becomes usable, qualified production?
- What limits installation after manufacturing: interconnection, permitting, construction, insurance, safety requirements, or financing?
- How many GW and GWh does each modeled portfolio require, and by when?
- Can production and deployment reach those volumes at the assumed price?
- When are transmission, flexible loads, pumped hydro, thermal storage, other long-duration storage, or firm generation more economical?

Expected outputs: learning-curve estimates with uncertainty, installed-system economics by duration, and a bottleneck map tied to the demand and generation scenarios. Avoid a blanket conclusion that batteries are either good or bad at storage.

PROMPT 4 — WHAT CHEAPER ENERGY ENABLES

4A. What becomes cheaper?
- Is the relevant metric equipment $/W, wholesale electricity, dependable delivered electricity $/MWh, or useful heat at a particular temperature?
- Does cheap power occur only at certain hours, or reliably around the clock?
- How much cost reduction is plausible, where, and by when? Treat orders-of-magnitude declines as a scenario to test, not an assumption.

4B. Which industries benefit, and at what threshold?
- What share of total cost is energy for manufacturing, computing, chemicals, metals, desalination, hydrogen, and synthetic fuels?
- Which need electricity, heat, fuels, feedstocks, or combinations?
- How does cheaper energy affect cost per unit of product after capital, labor, materials, logistics, and utilization?
- At what energy prices do specific processes become competitive?
- Which operations can follow variable supply or relocate near resources? What does flexibility cost?
- What constraints remain even with very cheap energy: equipment, materials, water, land, networks, communications, permits, or customers?

4C. What changes in markets and investment?
- What new demand becomes economical, and how does that feed back into the demand model?
- Which industries relocate or integrate with generation?
- Who captures the value: generators, technology suppliers, industrial operators, infrastructure owners, or customers?
- What market size, capital requirements, margins, and adoption timelines follow under each scenario?
- Which conclusions survive less favorable energy prices, lower utilization, or slower adoption?

Expected outputs: industry cost curves and competitiveness thresholds, flexible-demand opportunities, and an assessment of value capture. Cheaper energy does not automatically make the whole product cheaper by the same percentage.

MODELING CONVENTIONS AND SIMPLE STARTING EQUATIONS

These are methodological starting points, not completed models or predictions.

1. Keep units and accounting boundaries explicit.
   Power: W, MW, GW. Energy: Wh, MWh, TWh, EJ.
   1 TWh = 0.0036 EJ. A constant 1 GW for a 365-day year supplies 8.76 TWh.
   Primary energy, final energy, useful energy, electricity demand, and electricity generation are different quantities. Storage charging and network losses must be accounted for consistently.

2. Start with sector activity and intensity where data permit:
   D(r,s,t) = Activity(r,s,t) × EnergyIntensity(r,s,t)
   Total demand sums compatible regional/sector values.
   Electrification changes carriers and efficiencies; model that explicitly to avoid double counting.

3. Use simple growth curves only as transparent sensitivities:
   D(t) = D(base) × (1 + g)^(t - base)
   Label the assumed growth rate and explain its basis. An extrapolation is not an independent forecast.

4. Convert annual generation to illustrative installed capacity:
   Capacity_GW = AnnualGeneration_TWh / (8.76 × CapacityFactor)
   This equation alone cannot establish hourly reliability or firm capacity.
   Gross build requirements also include retirements and replacements; generation requirements include losses, storage losses, and curtailment where applicable.

5. Storage sizing needs power and duration:
   EnergyCapacity_MWh = DischargePower_MW × Duration_hours
   Nameplate sizing must additionally account for usable capacity, losses, reserve requirements, and degradation.

6. Learning curves:
   Cost(Q) = Cost(Q0) × (Q/Q0)^b
   Learning rate = 1 - 2^b
   Q is cumulative production measured consistently; b is estimated from data, not chosen to force a result.

7. Compare equivalent economic services.
   Use real currency in a stated base year; document location, financing, lifetime, utilization, and cost boundaries.
   Plant-level LCOE does not by itself measure the cost of a reliable delivered system.
   Distinguish observed costs, contract prices, company targets, model outputs, and forecasts.

8. Product economics:
   EnergyCostPerUnit = EnergyUsePerUnit × DeliveredEnergyPrice
   Add non-energy costs and any utilization effects. Test whether the claimed energy price is available when the process needs it.
